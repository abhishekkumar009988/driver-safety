━━━ FIRST, PROVE YOU STILL HAVE THE STORY ━━━
In eight lines: what this film is about · what physically happens
first to last · every character and what each wants · the turning
point · how it ends and what the audience feels · what they feel at
start, middle and end · the one image they must remember · the five
things that must not change.
If any of that is unclear in the STORY LOCK, say which line is
unclear and stop. Do not guess. Inventing is what makes the video
end up being a different film than the one I told you.

⚠️ THE STORY LOCK IS THE TRUTH. Translate it into shots. Never
improve it, reinterpret it, or add events. If a shot you want to
write is not in the event chain, you may not write it.

⭐ AND THE NEW RULE THAT GOVERNS THIS WHOLE PROMPT — THE TRACE LAW.
Every card must be traceable back to MY words. So every card opens
with a STORY LINE: a short quote from MY STORY or the STORY LOCK,
inside quotation marks — the exact line that produced this shot.

   · If a card has no line, it does not exist. Either delete it, or
     mark it clearly as PURE TRANSITION and keep it 3 seconds or less.
   · If two cards quote the same line, they must show DIFFERENT parts
     of it — one wide, one close — not the same moment twice.
   · Never upgrade my words. If I wrote "she looks at him", do not
     write "she gazes at him with tears, wind lifting her hair". My
     sentence is the ceiling, not the floor.
   · At the end of all the cards, print this table:

     | Shot | Story line it came from | Invented? |
       01   | "she turns from the door" | no
       02   | —                          | PURE TRANSITION
       03   | "the cloth slips"          | no

     Anything marked invented gets one line of justification, or it
     gets cut.

Veo and most tools give 8 second clips. 20 clips × 8s = 160s of
material for a 45s film — that is correct, you trim later.
Work out the shot count from the runtime and tell me the maths.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

① THE SHOT LIST — one row per shot, mapped to the event chain.
   Every chain event gets a shot; every shot maps to an event.
   Columns: # · ⭐ STORY LINE (my words, quoted) · chain event ·
   location · who is on screen · shot size · camera move ·
   ⭐ MOVEMENT THROUGH THE FRAME · ⭐ ENTERS FROM which edge ·
   ⭐ EXITS AT which edge · body state at start · body state at end ·
   what the face is doing · duration · CHAIN or CUT ·
   ⭐ ATMOSPHERE (the smoke/fog/dust/rain number for this shot).
   State the GENRE MODE from ⑩ above the table — it governs clip
   length, which moves are allowed and how the light behaves.
   Add a ⭐ MOVE column holding the exact named move from ⑧ for
   every shot. Read that column down: most rows must be Static,
   Dolly, Truck, Pan or Follow, and exactly ONE row may hold a bold
   move. If two bold moves appear, the film is noise — downgrade one.
   Plus two more columns: ⭐ WHAT IS BEHIND HER, and ⭐ FROM → TO
   (where her body starts and ends, with a rough distance for any
   travelling shot). A travelling shot whose FROM and TO are the same
   place is a walk on the spot — fix it at the list stage, before any
   prompt is written.
   Add a ⭐ SHOT TYPE column using ⑱: hand · eyes · detail ·
   silhouette · feet · back of head · wide · full body. Read it
   down: for every one wide or full-body row there should be about
   three detail rows. A list full of full-body rows will come out
   looking like AI however well it is written.
   Add a ⭐ HANDOFF column from ⑰ — at least two rows in the film
   must hand over through a moving object, and one of them at the
   hardest jump in place or time.
   Add a ⭐ BIG EFFECT column. Exactly ONE row in the whole table
   may be filled; every other row says none. Put it on the story's
   peak. A film with no spectacle shot is a film nobody forwards.
   Add a ⭐ FACE FROM → TO column: what the expression is at the
   start of the shot and what it has become by the end. If a row
   has the same word twice, that shot has no acting in it.
   State the film's one travel direction above the table.
   The MOVEMENT column can never be blank and the word "static" may
   appear at most ONCE in the whole table.
   Read the BEHIND HER column straight down. The same backdrop may
   not repeat more than twice in a row — that is the film standing
   still even while the character moves.

② WHO IS IN EACH SHOT — an exact name list per shot. Anyone not
   named may not appear. Nobody appears before their ARRIVES event.

③ THE DISTANCE PLAN — for every shot, how much of the frame height
   the figure fills. Use the ladder:
     EXTREME WIDE  under 1/10 — tiny, face unreadable, vast world
     WIDE          about 1/3 — full body with space around
     FULL          about 4/5 — head to feet
     MEDIUM        waist up — hands visible
     CLOSE-UP      head and shoulders — the eyes are the subject
     EXTREME CLOSE eyes only — pores visible
   Distance is meaning, not variety. Loneliness = wide, figure tiny.
   Feeling = close. Choose from the story, then say why.

④ THE CAMERA PLAN — pick every angle from the emotion:
     any reaction → CLOSE-UP, static
     strongest feeling → EXTREME CLOSE-UP, camera stops
     hiding something → CLOSE, slightly off-axis
     power or divinity → LOW ANGLE looking up
     shame or defeat → HIGH ANGLE looking down
     a relationship → TWO-SHOT or over-the-shoulder
     someone unobserved → shot from behind cover
     loneliness → WIDE, camera pulling away
   For every shot also state FACE ANGLE (full front / three-quarter
   / profile / from behind) and EYE HEIGHT (at eye level / below /
   above). If the meaning is in the face, it must be front or
   three-quarter — a profile hides half the performance, a wide
   hides all of it. Never leave height unstated; it renders flat.
   State the emotional reason for every angle. "Variety" is not one.
   Rules: no two neighbouring shots share a camera move or a size ·
   keep the camera on one side of the 180-degree line · screen
   direction holds · light stays on the same side · no more than 3
   shots in a row in one place.
   ⭐ AND USE 4 TO 6 CAMERA SETUPS FOR THE WHOLE FILM — see ㉕.

⑤ THE PERFORMANCE PLAN — the most important thing in this film.
   A small story with great faces works. A great story with blank
   faces does not.
   For every shot with a face, plan all six:
     INTENTION — what they are TRYING to do right now. A want, not
        a feeling. "to make him look away first."
     PUBLIC FACE — what they are deliberately showing.
     PRIVATE TRUTH — what is leaking underneath. The gap between
        these two IS the performance.
     MICRO-BEATS — the clip broken into 3 or 4 moments, each with a
        named muscle action. Also state whether the EYES move before
        the HEAD or the head before the eyes — that one detail is
        what reads as real acting.
     THE TELL — one involuntary detail: a swallow, fingers
        tightening, a late blink, a jaw setting, a curling foot.
     PLAYING TO — to him / away from him / to the group / to nobody,
        believing unobserved (the most powerful).

   INTENSITY — why anger comes out looking normal:
   Tools default every face to pleasant. Write "angry" and you get
   mildly displeased. Give every performance shot a number 1-10 and
   describe the muscles AT THAT LEVEL:
     ANGER 3  a flicker of irritation, eyes narrowing
     ANGER 6  jaw set, nostrils flaring, hard unblinking stare
     ANGER 9  face flushed and taut, teeth clenched behind closed
              lips, temple veins, body rigid, breathing hard through
              the nose, eyes locked and wet, not blinking at all
   Always write one level ABOVE what you want — the render always
   comes back one level below. Never let the emotion word stand
   alone; follow it with at least four muscles. Strong emotion needs
   a close-up. And say what it does to the BODY too — shoulders,
   hands, breathing, stillness or shaking. A face alone reads flat.

   BANNED, they produce dead faces: "smiles" · "looks happy" ·
   "is sad" · "reacts" · "shows emotion" · "divine expression".

   THE REACTION RULE: the event is not the drama, the FACE receiving
   it is. Every significant event needs two shots — the event, then
   the face. Cut to the reaction a beat late.

   ⭐ THE CHANGE RULE — the difference between an actor and a
   mannequin. A still frame can carry one expression. A CLIP cannot.
   If the face is the same at the first frame and the last frame,
   the audience sees a photograph being panned across, and they
   read it instantly as machine-made. So for every shot with a face,
   write the expression as a JOURNEY: where it starts, the exact
   second it turns, and where it ends. Then write the THOUGHT
   behind it in the character's own words — that is what fills the
   eyes. Then one involuntary LEAK. Then give the hands something
   to do. Those four are carried into the video prompt as BLOCK 2B.

   ⭐ AND SUPPRESS IT. Tools render emotion as performance: the
   mouth opens, the brows go up, the whole face announces itself.
   Real screen acting is the opposite — a person holding something
   down while one part of them gives it away. Write the holding
   down, and write the one place it escapes. A person trying not to
   cry reads real. A person crying reads like an AI.

⑥ TRAVEL THROUGH THE FRAME ⭐ THE BIGGEST FIX OF ALL
   A shot where a person stands in one place and only their hand, their
   earring or their hair moves is a DEAD SHOT. It reads as a photograph
   with a filter running on it. It will never cut together with the shot
   before or after it, however good the prompt is.

     ❌ DEAD: "she sits by the mirror and her earring sways"
     ❌ DEAD: "she stands under the tree and her dupatta lifts"
     ✅ ALIVE: "she rises and leaves the top of frame"
     ✅ ALIVE: "she enters frame left, crosses, exits frame right"
     ✅ ALIVE: "she walks toward the lens and passes it"

   So for EVERY shot, write one line called MOVEMENT THROUGH THE FRAME,
   chosen from these — the frame itself must change during the clip:
     she enters frame left and exits frame right
     she walks toward the camera and passes the lens
     she walks away from the camera and gets smaller
     she rises up out of the top of frame
     the camera tracks alongside her and the background streaks past
     the camera follows behind her as she pulls away
     the camera pushes in / pulls back / orbits, so the background moves
     her feet or hands travel through the frame and out of it

   HARD LIMIT: at most ONE shot in the entire film may stay still, and
   it must be the single biggest emotional close-up, where the feeling
   moves instead of the body. Every other shot travels. If your shot
   list has two static shots, you have a slideshow — fix it now.

   Also: a character must EXIT a place on screen and ENTER the next one
   on screen. The last second of a shot in place A should already be
   her leaving; the first second in place B should already be her
   arriving. Never let a shot simply begin with someone standing there.

   ⭐ AND TRAVELLING MEANS COVERING GROUND, NOT JUST MOVING
   There is a second kind of dead shot, and it is harder to spot: the
   character walks convincingly for the whole clip and ends up exactly
   where she started. Legs moving, hips moving, braid swinging — and
   the same wall behind her at the last frame as the first. A walk on
   a treadmill.

   It happens because the shot was planned as "she walks" instead of
   "she gets from here to there". A walk is a body doing something. A
   travelling shot is a body CHANGING PLACE. Only the second one moves
   a film forward.

   So plan every travelling shot as a JOURNEY WITH TWO ENDS:
       FROM: level with the blue door
       TO:   under the neem tree, about eight metres on
       PASSES ON THE WAY: the stacked clay pots, the tethered cow
   Then the beats can be written against real landmarks, and the
   audience can see how far she has come.

   Three rules that make ground-covering visible on screen:
     1. SOMETHING MUST LEAVE THE FRAME BEHIND HER. If nothing exits
        the back of the shot, she has not gone anywhere. Name the
        thing and the second it disappears.
     2. SOMETHING MUST GROW AHEAD OF HER. The tree, the gate, the
        ghat — small at 0s, large at the end. That growth IS the
        distance, as far as the viewer is concerned.
     3. HER SIZE OR POSITION IN FRAME MUST CHANGE, unless the camera
        is deliberately matching her pace. Walking away, she gets
        smaller. Walking past a locked camera, she crosses it and
        exits. Only a tracking camera holds her the same size — and
        then the BACKGROUND has to carry the whole job.

   Write the FROM, the TO and the metres on every travelling shot
   before you write a word of its prompt.

⑦ SCREEN DIRECTION ⭐ WHY TWO CLIPS MERGE OR CLASH
   This is the real editing rule behind "the frames don't feel joined".

   Pick ONE travel direction for the whole film — normally LEFT to RIGHT
   — and hold it. If she exits a shot at frame right, she enters the
   next shot from frame left, still travelling right. The eye then
   already knows where she is going before the new shot is understood,
   and the cut disappears.

   Break it and the audience feels she turned around for no reason —
   that is exactly the "alag-alag video" feeling.

   Rules:
   - State the direction once, at the top of the shot list, and put the
     entry edge and exit edge on every single shot row.
   - Never flip the direction unless you SHOW her turning around inside
     a shot, or you place a neutral shot (walking straight at or
     straight away from the lens) between the two sides.
   - The same applies to eyelines. If she looks off frame right in one
     shot, the thing she is looking at appears from frame left in the
     next.
   - Never cut between two camera positions less than 30 degrees apart.
     That is a jump cut and it reads as a mistake, not a cut.

⑧ THE CAMERA MOVE — USE THE REAL NAME ⭐ THE HIGGSFIELD METHOD
   There is a reason footage from camera-control platforms looks
   directed rather than drifting, and it is not a secret model. It
   is that every shot is assigned ONE NAMED, REAL CAMERA MOVE from a
   fixed list — the same words a camera operator says on a set.

   Those names are standard cinematography terms, so every video
   model already knows them from its training. Describing a move in
   your own words gets you drift. Naming it gets you the move.

     ❌ "the camera moves quickly towards her face"
     ✅ "crash zoom in on her face"

     ❌ "the camera goes around him in a circle"
     ✅ "360 orbit around him, moving left to right"

     ❌ "the camera follows her while the background stretches"
     ✅ "dolly zoom in — camera pushes forward while the lens zooms
        out, so she stays the same size and the background stretches
        behind her"

   Pick ONE move per shot from this list, write its exact name, then
   add the direction and the speed. Nothing else.

   ── EVERYDAY MOVES — these carry most of a film ──
     Static              camera locked off, subject moves in frame
     Dolly In / Out      camera travels toward or away, on a track
     Super Dolly In/Out  the same, longer and more dramatic
     Truck Left / Right  camera slides sideways, staying parallel
     Pan Left / Right    camera pivots in place, horizontally
     Tilt Up / Down      camera pivots in place, vertically
     Handheld            small natural shake, human and unstaged
     Follow / Head Tracking  camera holds behind or on the subject
                         at their pace as they travel

   ── MOVES WITH SHAPE — one or two in a film ──
     Crane Up / Down     camera rises or falls vertically, high arc
     Jib Up / Down       a shorter, gentler crane
     Arc Left / Right    camera sweeps a partial curve around
     360 Orbit           a full circle around the subject
     Lazy Susan          the subject turns, the camera holds
     Boom Down           camera descends, often following stairs
     Push to Glass       camera moves in until it meets a surface
     Through Object      camera passes through a gap, door or window

   ── BOLD MOVES — ⚠️ AT MOST ONE IN THE WHOLE FILM ──
     Crash Zoom In/Out   violent fast zoom, for one reaction or
                         reveal only
     Dolly Zoom (Vertigo) push in + zoom out together; subject fixed,
                         background warps. Dread, realisation.
     Bullet Time         time nearly frozen while the camera arcs
     Whip Pan            a blur-fast pan, usually hiding a cut
     Snorricam           camera locked to the body, world swings
     FPV Drone           fast flying path, dives and climbs
     YoYo Zoom           in then straight back out
     3D Rotation         the frame itself rotates

   ⚠️ THE DISCIPLINE THAT MATTERS MOST
   A bold move is a punch. Throw one and it lands. Throw six and the
   film is noise and nobody feels anything. Most shots in a good film
   are Static, Dolly, Truck, Pan or Follow. Reserve exactly ONE bold
   move for the single biggest beat, and write in the plan which shot
   gets it and why.

   NEVER stack two moves. "Orbit while zooming and craning up" gives
   you mush — the model picks one or blends them into drift. One
   named move, one direction, one speed.

   Every shot's move line is written like this:
       MOVE: Dolly In · slow · 1 metre of travel
       MOVE: Truck Right · matching her walking pace
       MOVE: Crash Zoom In · fast · on her eyes      ← the one bold
       MOVE: Static · locked off, she crosses the frame

   And the speed word is never optional. "Slow", "steady",
   "matching her pace", "fast" — a move with no speed renders at the
   model's default, which is usually too fast and reads as cheap.

⑨ SPEED RAMPS — where the film breathes
   Real action films do not run at one speed. They ramp: normal
   speed into slow motion at the peak, then back. Choose the ONE
   moment in this film that slows down, and state it:
       "Shot 11 ramps: normal speed for 0-2s, then slows to 40% for
        the turn, then returns to normal."
   One ramp per film. Two is a music video; three is a mess.

⑩ GENRE MODE — set it once, obey it everywhere
   A camera-control platform asks you to pick a genre before you
   generate anything, because genre decides pacing, light and how
   much the camera is allowed to move. Pick ONE for this film and
   write it at the top of the shot list. Then every shot obeys it.

     DRAMA    long takes, slow dolly, soft directional light,
              the camera waits on faces
     ACTION   shorter clips, handheld and follow moves, hard light,
              one crash zoom allowed
     EPIC     wide lenses, cranes and slow reveals, big landscape,
              low horizon
     NOIR     static and slow, hard single-source light, deep black
              shadow, the frame half dark
