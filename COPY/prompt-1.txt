═══ STEP E — BUILD THE STORY FROM ITS TRUE BEGINNING ═══

⚠️ Whatever I described is the MIDDLE of the story, never the start.
Build what came BEFORE (how everyone got there) and AFTER (where
everyone went). Then write a numbered EVENT CHAIN — every physical

Chain rules:
- Start from the earliest moment the audience needs to see.
- Every character's ENTRY is shown. Nobody appears from nowhere.
- Every character's EXIT is shown. Nobody vanishes.
- Every move from place A to place B is its own line.
- Every object tracked: where it came from, who holds it, where it
  ends up.
- CONNECTIVE BEATS: if a body must change state between two events,
  that change is its own event. Sitting in 5 and standing in 6?
  Then "she rises" is event 5b. Empty-handed then holding a lamp?
  "She picks up the lamp" is an event. Never skip these.

Then a CAST PRESENCE TIMELINE — one column per character, one row
per event, each cell exactly one of:
   ABSENT · ARRIVES (from where, how) · ON · OFF (where they are
   and what they are doing) · LEAVES (to where) · GONE
Hard rules: nobody goes ABSENT → ON without ARRIVES in between.
Nobody goes ON → GONE without LEAVES. A group arriving together is
still an arrival and still needs to be seen.
Then one line per character: "[name] enters at event [#] from
[where], leaves at event [#] to [where]." If you cannot fill that
line, the chain is incomplete — fix it.

Finally answer plainly. Any "not shown" means an event is missing:

  • How does each character arrive?

═══ STEP E2 — THE ROUTE (how the film travels) ═══

A film feels connected when the audience can WALK the route in their
head. Not "scene 1, scene 2" — but "she was here, then she went
through there, then she came out here."

Write THE ROUTE as one unbroken line of places:
   PLACE 1 → [how she leaves] → PLACE 2 → [how she leaves] → PLACE 3 ...

⚠️ THE THRESHOLD LAW — this is what makes scenes feel connected:
Between any two places there must be THREE shown beats, never two:
   1. LEAVING   — she is seen exiting place 1 (standing up, turning,
                  walking out of frame, reaching for the door)
   2. THRESHOLD — the in-between itself: the doorway, the gate, the
                  stairs, the path, the boat, the corner she turns.
                  This is a real physical action by a real character.
   3. ARRIVING  — she is seen entering place 2 (coming through the
                  gate, stepping onto the road, the light changing
                  on her face as the new place opens up)

Worked example so there is no confusion:
   ❌ NOT CONNECTED: she is getting ready at home → she is dancing
      on the road.  The audience is dropped. It feels like two
      different videos.
   ✅ CONNECTED: getting ready at home → she picks up her dupatta and
      turns to the door → she pulls the door open → she walks down
      the steps → her hand pushes the gate open → the gate swings and
      the road is revealed behind it → she steps onto the road →
      she stops, the music hits → she begins to dance.
   Same story. But now every scene grew out of the one before it.

Rules for the route:
- The film travels ONE WAY. Never return to a place unless the
  return is itself the story, and then it must look different.
- The THRESHOLD beat is never an empty cutaway of scenery. It is
  always a person doing a physical thing — a hand on a gate, feet on
  a step, a curtain pushed aside. If nobody is doing anything in
  it, delete it; it is filler.
- No place gets more than 3 shots in a row.
- Every threshold is a shot. Write it into the event chain now.

⭐ THE PROGRESS LAW — the cure for "the video isn't going anywhere"
A film can obey every rule above and STILL feel stuck. That happens
when the character moves a lot but never gets anywhere — she walks,
she turns, she walks again, all inside the same patch of ground, and
the audience sees the same walls behind her every time. Movement is
not progress. Progress is the BACKGROUND being different.

So the test is not "is she moving?" — it is "has the world behind
her changed?"

For every shot, write one line: WHAT IS BEHIND HER. Then read that
column straight down. If the same backdrop repeats more than twice in
a row, the film has stalled, however busy the character is.

Three ways to force real progress, use all three:

 1. THE BACKGROUND CHANGES EVERY SHOT OR TWO. She leaves the mud wall
    behind and now there are reeds. Then water. Then the far bank. If
    shot 7 could be swapped with shot 4 and nobody would notice, both
    shots are standing still.

 2. DISTANCE TO THE DESTINATION SHRINKS, VISIBLY. Decide the one place
    the whole film is heading toward — the tree, the gate, the river,
    the person. Then put it IN the frame, getting closer:
      shot 3  — a shape far off through the reeds
      shot 6  — the tree now fills a third of the frame behind her
      shot 9  — she is under its branches
    The audience cannot feel progress they cannot see. Give them a
    landmark and let it grow.

 3. THE GROUND ITSELF CHANGES. Swept earth, then dust, then wet soil,
    then river sand. Underfoot texture is the cheapest and strongest
    signal that she has genuinely travelled.

Also: never give two neighbouring shots the same shot size AND the
same camera move. Wide-tracking, then wide-tracking, reads as one
long stuck moment even when the location has changed.

Print a PROGRESS TABLE before the shot list:
   shot # · place · what is behind her · how far to the destination
Then confirm in one line: "The background changes at least every two
shots, and the destination is visibly closer each time it appears."
If it is not true, add the missing shots now.

Print the route, then confirm: "Every place change in this film has
a LEAVING, a THRESHOLD and an ARRIVING." If one is missing, add the
event and renumber the chain.

  neck, eyes and brow only · 5 whole frame, silhouette only
  fog 0 none · 1 ankle · 2 knee · 3 waist · 4 chest · 5 full frame
  dust 1 motes in a beam · 2 off the ground under footsteps ·
  3 the beam itself visible · 4 the air greys out, distance lost
  rain 1 few slow drops · 2 steady, visible on dark cloth ·
  3 heavy, water standing on the ground · 4 downpour, faces unusable

⭐ THE RULE THAT MATTERS: in any shot where the audience must READ
the face, nothing in front of it goes above 2. Levels 4 and 5 are
for silhouettes, wides and object shots only.

    water standing on the ground"

Nothing in this film may be described later as "a little smoke" or
"some fog". It is a number, chosen now.
